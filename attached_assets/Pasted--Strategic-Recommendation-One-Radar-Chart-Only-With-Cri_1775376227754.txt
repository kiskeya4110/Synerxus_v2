### Critical Design Constraints:
| Requirement | Specification | Why |
|-------------|---------------|-----|
| **Monochrome only** | Navy (#0A2463) lines on off-white (#F9FAFB) | Avoids "marketing" perception |
| **Explicit labels** | Every axis labeled with exact score (no guessing) | Auditors need precision |
| **Always paired with table** | Radar appears *below* the weighted scoring table | Table is primary source; radar is supplemental |
| **No fill color** | Outline only (no shaded interior) | Prevents visual distortion of area perception |
| **5 axes max** | One per scoring dimension | More axes become unreadable |

## ⚠️ Why Radar Charts Are Generally Problematic for Auditors

| Issue | Impact |
|-------|--------|
| **Area distortion** | Human eye misjudges area enclosed by polygon—92 vs 85 looks more different than it is |
| **Axis order manipulation** | Reordering axes changes visual impression (ethical risk) |
| **Poor for precise comparison** | Auditors need exact numbers—not visual estimation |
| **Feels "marketing-y"** | Associated with executive dashboards—not audit evidence |

## 💡 Final Recommendation

**Include ONE radar chart ONLY if:**
1. You're showcasing the Verified Innovation Scaling Index scoring methodology  
2. It appears *after* and *supplemental to* the weighted scoring table  
3. It follows monochrome minimalist styling with explicit axis labels  
4. You add a disclaimer that the table—not the chart—is the audit source  

**Otherwise, skip radar charts entirely.** The weighted scoring table alone is more auditor-friendly, defensible, and professional. Radar charts add visual interest but reduce precision—the opposite of what auditors need.

> **Bottom line:** In verification reporting, *precision beats aesthetics*. Use radar charts sparingly—if at all—and never as the primary evidence source.
